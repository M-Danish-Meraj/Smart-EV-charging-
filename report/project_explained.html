Chapter 1

The story in one minute

Imagine Karachi in the future. Lots of people drive electric cars, and electric cars need to be charged, the way a phone needs charging. So the city wants to set up charging stations.

The city has two puzzles:

  1. Which roads should connect the charging places so that everything is linked, while spending as little money as possible?
  2. Which cars should each station charge when there is not enough electricity for everyone?

We solved the first puzzle with a greedy method (always grab the cheapest good thing next) and the second with dynamic programming (break a big question into small ones and remember the answers). Then we tested everything, ran experiments, and built a dashboard to show the results.

Like you're ten

Think of a big school. Puzzle one: draw paths between classrooms using the least paint, so every classroom can be reached. Puzzle two: there are only a few lunchboxes of sweets, each with a size limit, and many children who want sweets. Who gets sweets so the most children are happy?

Chapter 2

What was the problem?

Puzzle A: connecting places with roads

We have 12 places (we call them sites) and 24 roads between them. Every road has a cost (how much it costs to use or build it). We want to pick roads so that every place we can reach is connected, and the total cost is as small as possible.

What makes it tricky:

  • Some roads are closed (unavailable). We are not allowed to use them, even if they are cheap.
  • Because of closed roads, some places are cut off, like little islands. We can't magically connect them.
  • Later, roads can break. We need to fix our road plan quickly.
  • Sometimes two roads cost exactly the same. We need a fair, fixed rule to choose between them, so the answer is always the same.

Puzzle B: sharing limited electricity

We have 24 cars asking for a charge (we call them requests) and 3 working stations. Each station can only give out a fixed amount of energy. Each car brings a benefit score (how good it is to serve that car).

  • There is more demand than energy, so some cars must be left out.
  • A car needs its whole charge. Half a charge doesn't count.
  • A car can only go to one station, and only to a station it can actually reach by road.
  • Some cars want more energy than any station has. They can never be served.
Like you're ten

You have three backpacks of different sizes and a pile of toys. Each toy has a size and a "fun score". You can't cut a toy in half, and a toy can only go in one backpack. Which toys do you pack to get the most fun without the backpacks bursting?

Chapter 3

What were the goals?

  1. Connect every reachable place with the cheapest set of roads, never using a closed road.
  2. When roads break, repair the plan and prove the repair is just as cheap as starting over.
  3. Give energy to cars to get the highest total benefit, without overfilling any station and without giving one car two stations.
  4. For every car that is not served, say why.
  5. Prove the methods are correct and measure how fast they are.
  6. Run experiments: roads breaking, important places removed, less energy, more cars, bigger cities.
  7. Show everything in a dashboard that anyone can explore.

The course marks this out of 20: 8 for working, tested code; 6 for proofs and speed analysis; 4 for explaining trade-offs; 2 for matching each problem to the right method.

Chapter 4

Our pretend city: Karachi-EV-12

We didn't have real data, so we made a pretend (synthetic) city. The place names are real Karachi areas, but every cost, score and size is made up. We say "synthetic" everywhere so nobody thinks these are real numbers.

A small computer program (scripts/gen_dataset.py) writes the city into four simple spreadsheet files (CSV). It uses a fixed "random seed" (363, our course number), so it makes exactly the same city every time.

Why some places matter more: severity

Every place has a severity number from 2 to 10. Think of it as "how important is this place". Gulshan-e-Iqbal has 10 and DHA has 9, the two highest. Saddar is the hub (the main centre), and we deliberately gave it a middle score so it is not one of the top two.

Energy units, and why we round

Computers find whole numbers easier, so we count energy in units, where 1 unit = 10 kWh (kWh is how electricity is measured).

  • A car's request rounds up: a car wanting 21 kWh needs 3 units. We'd rather give a tiny bit extra than not enough.
  • A station's capacity rounds down: a station with 125 kWh offers 12 units. We never promise energy it doesn't have.
Like you're ten

If a toy is 2½ blocks long, you need a 3-block space for it. If a backpack holds 12½ blocks, you can only count on 12 full blocks.

The city was built on purpose

We designed the city so every tricky case shows up:

  • Three islands. The 4 closed roads are exactly the ones joining Malir, Airport and Landhi to the rest. Surjani Town has no roads at all. So there are 3 groups: the big main group (8 places), the cut-off group (3 places) and Surjani alone.
  • A triangle of cheap roads (Saddar, Clifton, DHA) to show how we avoid going in circles.
  • Roads with the same cost to test the fair tie-breaking rule.
  • A closed road that is the cheapest of all (Korangi to Landhi, cost 2), to prove we really ignore closed roads.
  • Too much demand: in the main group, cars want 37 units but stations hold only 21.
  • Two giant cars: EV08 needs 14 units (no station is that big) and EV20 needs 9 units (Malir's station holds only 7).

A test checks all of these properties automatically, so the city can never quietly change.

Chapter 5 · Part A

Connecting places: the greedy way

For roads we used Kruskal's algorithm. It is called greedy because at every step it grabs the cheapest road that is still useful, and never changes its mind.

How Kruskal works, step by step

  1. Throw away closed roads before doing anything else. They are not allowed, so we don't even look at them.
  2. Line up the remaining roads from cheapest to most expensive. If two roads cost the same, the one touching more important places (higher total severity) goes first. If that is also a tie, we go by the place codes (S01, S02, …) so the order is always the same.
  3. Walk down the line. For each road, ask: "Does this road join two places that are not already connected?"
    • Yes: keep it.
    • No: skip it, because it would only make a loop (a circle), which wastes money.
  4. Stop at the end. What we kept is the cheapest possible road plan.

The "team badge" trick (Union-Find)

How does the computer know quickly whether two places are already connected? It gives every place a team badge. At the start every place is its own team. When we keep a road, the two teams merge into one. To check a road, we just ask: "Do these two places wear the same badge?" If yes, the road would make a loop.

Two small tricks make this super fast: merge the smaller team into the bigger one (union by rank), and when you look up a badge, write the answer down so next time is instant (path compression).

DSU = Disjoint Set Union = Union-FindMST = minimum spanning treeMSF = minimum spanning forest

Tree or forest?

If all places can be joined, the result is one connected tree of roads. Our city has islands, so we get one small tree per island. Several trees together are called a forest. We never invent a road between islands.

Why being greedy is safe here

Normally being greedy can trick you. Here it is proven safe: if you split the places into two sides, the cheapest road crossing between them can always be part of a cheapest plan. If some "best plan" didn't use it, you could swap it in for a more expensive crossing road and the plan would get cheaper or stay the same. Kruskal always picks exactly that cheapest crossing road. This is called the cut property.

Fixing broken roads (incremental repair)

If a road in our plan breaks, we don't have to start from zero:

  1. Remove the broken road. Its tree splits into two halves.
  2. Look at every remaining open road and find the cheapest one that joins the two halves.
  3. If one exists, add it. If none exists, the island really has split in two.
  4. If the broken road was not in our plan at all, nothing needs fixing.

We always check the repaired plan against a full Kruskal restart to prove it costs exactly the same.

Chapter 6

The island rule: how the two puzzles connect

A car can only charge at a station it can drive to. In our model that means the station must be on the same island (same connected group) as the place the car starts from.

  • A car in Saddar can use Clifton's station (ST1) or Gulshan's station (ST2), both in the main group.
  • A car in Malir, Airport or Landhi can only use Malir's station (ST3).
  • A car in Surjani Town can't reach any station.

So puzzle A (roads) decides who is allowed to use which station, and puzzle B (energy) decides who actually gets energy. That is the link between the two halves of the project.

Before sharing energy, we label the cars that can never be served:

  • No eligible station: no station on its island (Surjani's car, EV24).
  • Too large: bigger than every station it could reach (EV08 and EV20).
  • Origin removed: its home place was deleted (only in experiment E3).

Any car that could fit but still doesn't get energy is labelled Capacity competition: others were a better use of the limited energy.

Chapter 7 · Part B

Sharing energy: dynamic programming

Why not just be greedy again?

Here greedy can be fooled. Say a station holds 50 units:

  • Car A needs 50 units and brings 100 points.
  • Cars B and C need 20 units each and bring 60 points each.

Greedy grabs the biggest prize, car A: 100 points. But B and C together fit and give 120 points. Greedy lost. We needed a method that never misses the best answer.

The backpack puzzle (knapsack)

This is a famous puzzle called the knapsack problem. Ours has several backpacks (stations), so it is the multiple knapsack problem. Every car is all-or-nothing, which makes it the 0/1 version.

How dynamic programming solves it

Like you're ten

Imagine a notebook. On each page you write: "If the backpacks still had this much space, and I had only looked at the first few toys, the best fun I could get is this." You fill the pages one toy at a time. For each new toy you ask just two questions: is it better to skip this toy, or to put it in one of the backpacks that has room? You look up the earlier page to answer. When you reach the last toy, the answer is waiting on the last page.

The grown-up version:

  • The question (state): best score using the first i cars when station 1 has c₁ units left, station 2 has c₂, station 3 has c₃.
  • The starting page: with no cars, the score is 0.
  • The choice for each car: skip it, or give it to one eligible station that still has room (score goes up by the car's benefit, that station's space goes down by the car's size).
  • The answer: the page for "all cars, all stations full size".
  • Who got what: we wrote down every choice, so we walk backwards from the answer and read off which car went where (backtracking).

Because each car is decided once, and the choice is "skip" or "exactly one station", a car can never be given two stations.

How big is the notebook?

Station sizes are 12, 9 and 7 units. Each can have 0 to 12, 0 to 9, or 0 to 7 units left, so one page per car has 13 × 10 × 8 = 1,040 boxes. With 21 cars going into the DP, plus the starting page, that is 22 × 1,040 = 22,880 boxes, about 206 KB of memory. A computer fills that in well under a millisecond.

Add a 4th station (Korangi, 5 units) and each page grows 6 times to 6,240 boxes. That is why we keep the number of stations small: every new station multiplies the notebook size.

Tie rule

If two choices give the same score, we prefer the one that serves more cars. If it's still a tie, we prefer the lower station number. This keeps the answer the same every time.

Chapter 8

How we know it's right

1. Brute force: try every single possibility

For small puzzles (up to 10 cars), a second program simply tries every possible way to place the cars, checks which ones fit, and keeps the best. It is slow but impossible to fool. With 3 stations each car has 4 choices (skip, ST1, ST2, ST3), so 10 cars give 410 = 1,048,576 ways.

We made 200 random small puzzles and solved each with both methods. They agreed 200 out of 200 times.

For our real city with 24 cars, brute force would need 424 ≈ 280 trillion tries. That is why we need dynamic programming.

2. Checkers (validators)

After every run, separate checker code re-checks the answer from scratch:

  • Roads: no closed road used, no loops, every island fully connected, cost added up again.
  • Energy: no car used twice, no station overfilled, every car at a station it can reach, total score added up again.

3. Automatic tests

We wrote 333 automatic tests. They check the city's design, bad input files getting rejected, the road plan matching a well-known library (used only in tests), the tie rules, repairs on 60 random cities, and more. All 333 pass.

4. The greedy shortcut, for comparison

We also built a quick greedy shortcut for energy (best points per unit first). On our city it scored 781 against the DP's 815. On 139 random puzzles it was perfect 97 times but lost 6.2% on average and up to 58.8% in the worst case. So it's fast but can't be trusted to be best.

Chapter 9

The five experiments

One program, run_all.py, runs all experiments and writes every number to files. We never type a result by hand.

E1 · Baseline: the normal city

Everything as designed. Result: 9 roads with cost 36 and 3 islands; 12 of 24 cars served, benefit 815, all 28 units used. In a bonus "stress run" we switched on the spare station at Korangi (k = 4). Then 14 cars are served and the benefit rises to 863, but the notebook grows from 1,040 to 6,240 boxes per page.

E2 · Three roads break

We picked three roads before the experiment, each one testing a different case:

  • R06 PECHS–Gulshan was in our plan, and another road can replace it. Repair adds R11 PECHS–North Nazimabad (cost 6).
  • R20 Airport–Landhi was in our plan, and nothing can replace it. Landhi becomes its own island.
  • R07 Clifton–PECHS was not in our plan, so nothing changes.

The repaired plan costs 33, exactly what a full restart gives, with the very same roads. Benefit drops to 773 because Landhi's cars lost their station.

E3 · Remove the two most important places

We deleted Gulshan-e-Iqbal and DHA (severity 10 and 9), all 11 of their roads, and the station at Gulshan. Then we ran both puzzles again.

  • The road cost drops to 27 (fewer places to connect), but there are now 4 islands: Gulistan-e-Johar's only road went through Gulshan, so Johar is cut off.
  • Total station space drops from 28 to 19 units. Only 8 cars are served, benefit 540.

Lesson: removing an important place can split the map and take a station with it. Note that Kruskal only uses severity to break ties; it does not try to protect important places.

E5 · Bigger cities and bigger puzzles

We made random cities of 20, 50, 100 and 200 places and timed everything (the middle value of 20 runs, called the median).

  • Repair vs restart: on 200 places, repairing 3 broken roads took about 0.33 ms, against about 1.03 ms for a full Kruskal restart. Both always gave the same cost. We measured this instead of assuming it.
  • Notebook growth: doubling the station sizes again and again (×1, ×2, ×4, ×8) grew the notebook from 1,040 to 403,617 boxes per page, and memory from 234 KB to 90.8 MB. Bigger numbers mean a bigger notebook. That is what "pseudo-polynomial" means.
  • Brute force vs DP: at 10 cars, brute force tried 1,048,576 ways in about 1.6 seconds; DP took about 0.24 ms.

Timings depend on the computer and change slightly on every run. The dashboard always shows the latest run.

The three tricky cases the course asks for

The course says three special cases must run in our code with real printed output. They are saved in output/edge_cases.log and shown in the dashboard.

  1. Disconnected map. Closed roads R21–R24 are thrown away first. We get a forest of 9 roads over 3 islands, each listed by name, and Surjani Town is marked "isolated". No fake road is invented.
  2. Too much demand. The main group wants 37 units but has 21; the cut-off group wants 15 but has 7. Every station stays within its limit, and the 9 cars that lost out are listed.
  3. A car that's too big. EV08 needs 14 units but its stations hold 12 and 9. EV20 needs 9 but Malir holds 7. Both are labelled "too large" and never assigned.

We also show: the loop being skipped, the tie rule, the "one car, one station" guard, zero cars, a zero-size station, and a repair with one broken road inside the plan and one outside.

Chapter 11

The steps we took, in order

  1. Set up the project. Folders, the rules file (CLAUDE.md), the list of tools needed, and the project PDFs.
  2. Built the pretend city and a test that checks every design property. We found the draft plan's "about 45 units of demand" was not enough to create real over-demand, so we raised it.
  3. Roads part: team badges (Union-Find), reading and checking the files, throwing away closed roads, and Kruskal. Tested against a trusted library.
  4. Energy part: the island rule and the DP notebook with backtracking.
  5. Brute force, and a test proving DP agrees with it on 151 random puzzles.
  6. Road repair, tested against full Kruskal on our city and 60 random cities.
  7. Checkers and experiments: run_all.py runs E1 to E5 and writes all tables and charts.
  8. The edge-case log with the three required cases.
  9. Extras: the greedy shortcut for comparison, the charts, and finally the dashboard website.
  10. Report support: a notes file with step-by-step recipes (pseudocode), proofs and speed analysis, plus all figures and results copied into report/.

Each step was saved separately in Git and pushed to GitHub, so the history shows how the project grew.

Chapter 14

What this project does not do

  • The data is pretend. It can't tell you real costs or real demand in Karachi.
  • The cheapest road plan is cheapest in cost only, not in travel time, safety or coverage.
  • Benefit scores are our choice. Highest total benefit doesn't mean the fairest sharing.
  • Rounding to 10 kWh blocks can make a car look slightly bigger than it is.
  • The notebook grows very fast with more stations, so exact DP works for 3 or 4 stations, not 50.
  • Queues, charger speed, prices, power-grid limits and charging time are not modelled.
  • It decides how to share energy at the stations we have; it does not decide where to build new ones.

Chapter 15

How to run it yourself

  1. Install the Python tools: pip install -r requirements.txt
  2. Run all the tests: python -m pytest (expect 333 passed)
  3. Run all experiments: python run_all.py (results go to output/)
  4. Copy results to the report and dashboard: python scripts/build_report_assets.py
  5. Start the dashboard: npm --prefix dashboard install, then npm --prefix dashboard run dev, and open http://localhost:3000
